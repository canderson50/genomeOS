## TL;DR

Two of our data sources had never had their terms read and written down. Now they have. Both allow
commercial use of what we publish, so nothing is blocked and nothing has to be removed.

The inventory that lists non-commercial data now reports **zero unchecked sources**, where before it
listed two.

One surprise came out of reading gnomAD's terms, and it matters: they say *some* of their bundled
annotations carry separate restrictions, and name the one we already knew about only as an
**example**. So our list of known restricted fields can catch a repeat, but never a first
occurrence. That is now written down rather than assumed.

## What the sources actually say

Both pages were read on **2026-09-15** and quoted word for word in
`docs/audits/gnomad-dbsnp-commercial-use.md`, so anyone can check the finding without revisiting the
sites.

**gnomAD — `explicitly_open`.** Its primary exome and genome data carry a Creative Commons Zero
dedication, which it describes as usable "for any purpose without legally having to give
attribution." That is an explicit licence permitting commercial use.

**dbSNP — `no_restriction_found`, deliberately not `explicitly_open`.** NCBI states it "places no
restrictions on the use or distribution of the data" and strengthens that by refusing submissions
that arrive with restrictions attached. But it then says in plain words that it "cannot provide
comment or unrestricted permission", because submitters may hold rights and NCBI has none to
transfer.

Those are different findings and the difference is the whole point of having two words for it. A
completed check that found no restriction is not a grant. Recording `explicitly_open` here would
claim permission the source explicitly says it cannot give, which is the kind of plausible-sounding
metadata the publication-evidence safeguards exist to prevent.

## The part that changes how the marking works

From gnomAD's terms, emphasis mine:

> **Some** annotations may have restrictions on usage. **For instance,** SpliceAI annotations have
> been computed by Illumina and are provided with permission under a CC BY NC 4.0 license for
> academic and non-commercial use. It is the responsibility of users to abide by all relevant
> licensing requirements.

Not a closed list, and the burden is explicitly on us. So the tripwire in the exporter catches a
repeat of a restriction someone already found, and cannot catch the first occurrence of a new one. A
field arriving in a payload for the first time needs its own check. That is now stated in
`docs/non-commercial-data.md` instead of being left as an assumption a reader might not make.

I also checked what our gnomAD payload actually carries. No SpliceAI field is present. The
annotations that are there are ClinVar, a consequence prediction, and gnomAD's own constraint
metric, none of which is a known non-commercial annotation.

## A gate bug this exposed

The privacy check refused the commit, reporting the NCBI citation URL as a personal file path.
NCBI publishes its policy under a route beginning `/home/about/`, and the pattern read that as a home
directory belonging to a user called "about".

I fixed the detector rather than working around it. The alternative was truncating a citation URL to
satisfy a false positive, which trades real evidence for a passing gate and teaches the next person
to do the same. A `/home/` or `/Users/` segment inside a URL is a web route, not a path pinned to
someone's machine. The exemption is scoped to the URL span, so a genuine path sitting beside a link
on the same line is still caught — there is a test for exactly that.

This is in the same pull request because it blocked it, not as a drive-by improvement.
