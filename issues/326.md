## Body

## TL;DR

We are considering five large Chinese genomic resources. Nobody has checked whether they contain
some of the same people.

If they do, and we combine them, we would count those individuals twice and publish an allele
frequency that is simply wrong. Nothing in our current pipeline would notice.

This needs answering before any two of them are merged. Either one alone is safe.

---

## The five

| Resource | Institution | Cohort character |
|---|---|---|
| PGG.Han | Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health | Multi-site aggregation of existing GWAS cohorts |
| ChinaMAP | Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai | Metabolic cohorts — REACTION, CDC surveillance |
| WBBC | Westlake University, Hangzhou | Own recruitment |
| NyuWa | Institute of Biophysics, CAS, Beijing | Own recruitment |
| CMDB | BGI | Non-invasive prenatal testing residuals, female only |

## What we know, and what we do not

A read of each paper's data-availability and recruitment sections found **no statement of shared
cohorts**, and the five come from visibly distinct institutions. That is mildly reassuring and
nothing more.

**Absence of a stated overlap is not evidence of independent recruitment.** Papers rarely enumerate
which other consortium already holds their samples, and PGG.Han is explicitly an aggregation of
pre-existing cohorts, which is exactly the shape that swallows other people's samples.

One near-miss worth recording so nobody repeats it: NyuWa's paper compares its variant catalogue to
ChinaMAP's and reports 27.3% overlap across about 959,000 cancer-gene variants. That is a
**variant-catalogue** comparison. It says nothing about shared participants, and it would be easy to
misread as reassurance.

## Why this specifically breaks a frequency atlas

Double-counting inflates the denominator and the numerator together, so the point estimate can look
plausible while the uncertainty is badly understated. A surface fitted on it would be confidently
wrong, which is worse than being visibly uncertain. Our masking rules cannot catch it because
nothing in the data says these are the same people.

## What would actually answer it

Not another literature search. Concretely:

1. Read each cohort's constituent-study citations and look for shared underlying cohorts,
   especially anything PGG.Han aggregates.
2. Check whether any two report the same recruitment centres in the same years.
3. Where the papers are silent, ask the authors. This is a reasonable and normal question, and the
   answer is worth more than any inference we could make.
4. Failing all that, treat the pair as potentially overlapping and refuse the merge, which is a
   valid output.

## Interim rule

**Ingest any one of these alone. Do not merge two of them into a shared surface until this is
answered.** #325 ingests WBBC on its own and is safe under that rule.
