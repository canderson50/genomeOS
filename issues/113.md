## Body

Partial national coverage has two possible answers, and #94 implements both so they can be
compared rather than argued about. One of them is in tension with an invariant, which is why this
issue exists rather than a commit.

## The two methods

**Method A — `burden.national.national_totals` (the default).** Aggregate over supported cells
only, and refuse any country whose population-weighted mapped fraction falls below
`MIN_MAPPED_POPULATION_FRACTION` (0.80). This is AGENTS.md and §4/§10 as written: *"masked cells
(`unknown`, `prior_dominated`) are excluded from every aggregation statistic, and the excluded
fraction is returned with the result."*

**Method B — `burden.national.national_totals_propagating_masked` (an experiment).** Aggregate
over every populated cell, masked ones included, carrying each cell's full posterior draws
through the burden expression and taking quantiles of the national total afterwards. Nothing is
imputed and no point value is substituted. The argument for it: a masked cell's posterior has by
construction barely moved off the prior, so it contributes a *wide* distribution and the national
interval widens roughly in proportion to the unmapped population share. Every country gets a
number; a badly covered country gets an interval wide enough to be self-evidently untrustworthy.

## Why this needs a decision rather than a merge

**Method B does not satisfy the invariant as written.** Masked cells are inside its aggregation
statistic. The invariant's purpose — never presenting an inferred value as a measured one — is
arguably still served, because what enters is the cell's whole posterior rather than a point
estimate, and the width is carried through to the answer. But that is a reinterpretation of the
invariant, and reinterpreting an invariant is a decision for the project, not for a PR.

Three specific things to weigh:

1. **The widening argument rests on the caller's draws.** Method B is honest exactly when masked
   cells' draws really are near-prior. They are for a posterior sampled at those cells; they are
   not if the draws were narrowed, clipped or filled upstream — and the function cannot tell.
2. **A wide interval is not the same as a refusal.** A wide number still renders as a number, on
   a map, in a table, in a citation. The §4 case is that a rendered value is read as a claim
   regardless of what its interval says.
3. **It changes what a burden layer means.** Under Method B, a country's total includes people
   whose local frequency we never measured, so the layer is no longer "burden where we have
   evidence" but "burden plus prior-driven extrapolation, with an interval that says so".

## Comparison numbers

Both were run through `score_parity` in #94, but on **synthetic** inputs — a real comparison
needs a real fit and a real population raster (#112 blocks the first). On that stand-in:

| | Method A (`supported_only`) | Method B (`propagate_masked`) |
|---|---|---|
| countries estimated (of 191 published) | 111 | 165 |
| point inside published IQR | 9.4% | 12.0% |
| intervals overlap | 11.0% | 14.7% |
| global total within published IQR | PASS | FAIL |

Method B scores better on criteria 1 and 2 simply by answering more often, and worse on 3.
**These numbers describe an invented field and settle nothing** — they show only that the
comparison is measurable and that the two methods diverge where you would expect. Re-run on a
real fit before deciding anything.

One caveat about the stand-in that would matter for a real comparison: its per-cell draws are
independent, while a real GP posterior is spatially correlated. Summing independent cells averages
their uncertainty away, so Method B's characteristic widening is understated here.

## What is needed

A decision on whether Method B may ever produce published artifacts, and if so under what caveats
(a mandatory coverage column, a distinct layer name, a UI treatment). Until then it stays reachable
only by its own function, no default reaches it, and the AGENTS.md invariant text is unchanged.

Related: #94, #38 (the mask), #92, #112.
